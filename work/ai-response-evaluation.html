01 / THE CHALLENGE

What makes an
answer better?

When two responses sound convincing, selecting the better one means looking past tone or length. Does it answer the question correctly? Follow the instructions? Explain itself clearly?

I created an independent practice case study to explore how a consistent set of criteria can turn subjective preferences into explainable comparisons.

All 20 response pairs were author-created hypothetical examples; they are not captured model outputs.

02 / THE METHOD

Build a repeatable
rubric.

Each response is assessed against five criteria. A criterion scores 0 when it fails, 1 when it partially meets the requirement, and 2 when it fully meets it. Five applicable criteria give a maximum of 10 points.

01 · Accuracy

Is the content factually correct?

02 · Reasoning

Is the explanation logical and supported?

03 · Instruction adherence

Does the answer meet explicit constraints?

04 · Completeness

Does it cover what was requested?

05 · Clarity

Is it understandable and unambiguous?

When a criterion genuinely does not apply, it can be marked N/A and the score normalized. The goal is consistency, not rewarding length for its own sake.

03 / AN EXAMPLE

Small prompts.
Important details.

One illustrative case tests whether a response can distinguish a logical statement from its converse:

ILLUSTRATIVE CASE 04 / REASONING“All roses are flowers. Does that imply all flowers are roses?”
RESPONSE A · PREFERRED ✓No, the converse does not follow.
RESPONSE BYes, because roses are flowers.

Evaluation note: The second answer commits a logical error. The fact that every rose is a flower does not imply that every flower is a rose.

This is a demonstration of evaluator reasoning using invented responses. It is not an estimate of real AI model accuracy.
